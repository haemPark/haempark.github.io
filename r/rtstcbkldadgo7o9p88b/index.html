Part 01

Introduction

1.1Motivation

Automated visual inspection is an important component of semiconductor manufacturing, where inspection systems are used to identify defects before defective parts propagate to later and more costly stages of production. From a machine-learning perspective, the problem can be formulated as anomaly detection: given a chip image, determine whether an anomalous pattern is present and, when spatial annotation is available, identify where the anomaly occurs.

A central difficulty of this problem is the strong imbalance between normal and anomalous samples. Normal chip images can be collected in large quantities, whereas images containing a particular defect type are much less frequent. As a result, a practical dataset may contain thousands of normal samples but only a few number of examples for an individual anomaly category. The standard public benchmarks exhibit the same regime, with a few dozen annotated defects per category against a far larger nominal set 12.

Training a discriminative detector directly under this imbalance creates two related problems. First, the training objective can be dominated by the normal class, causing the learned classifier to underemphasize rare anomalies34. Second, and more fundamentally, a few dozen anomalous samples cover only a small region of the support of the anomalous distribution. The model cannot learn the pattern of defects it has never observed. Reweighting or resampling can change the influence of the observed anomalous samples, but it cannot introduce anomaly patterns that are absent from the training set.

Detail — mechanisms by which imbalance degrades training

Majority bias in the empirical objective. With N normal and M anomalous samples, empirical risk minimization optimizes

R(θ) = πn En[ ℓ(fθ(x), n) ]  +  πa Ea[ ℓ(fθ(x), a) ],    πa = M/(N+M)

where N >> M, standard empirical risk minimization assigns substantially more total contribution to the normal class unless class balancing is introduced. Consequently, a model can obtain a low average training loss while still performing poorly on the anomalous class. This also makes aggregate accuracy an insufficient metric when the class ratio is highly skewed. Buda et al.3 show empirically across CNN architectures that the effect grows with the imbalance ratio and with task difficulty, and that accuracy is an inadequate metric in this regime. Johnson and Khoshgoftaar4 survey the deep-learning literature on the same problem.

Prior shift at the decision threshold. A model trained under empirical prior πa and deployed under a different operating prior requires threshold correction; the learned scores are calibrated to the training prior, not the deployment one.

Representation versus classifier. Kang et al.8 decompose long-tailed recognition and find that imbalance harms the classifier far more than the learned representation, and that re-balancing the classifier alone recovers much of the loss. This motivates class balancing and classifier-level remedies for the first problem above, but such techniques still operate only on the examples already present in the dataset.

What re-balancing cannot address. Cost-sensitive losses56, over-sampling and SMOTE-style interpolation7 all modify the effective class prior. None enlarges the support of the empirical anomalous distribution. Repeatedly presenting the same small anomaly set can therefore improve class balance3 while still leaving substantial gaps in the variation of anomalies observed during training.

Existing approaches to class imbalance can be broadly divided into data-level and algorithm-level methods. Data-level methods increase the effective contribution of the minority class through over-sampling or image augmentation 7, while algorithm-level methods modify the training objective through cost-sensitive losses, focal losses5, or class-balanced weighting6. These techniques can reduce majority-class bias, but they remain limited by the anomaly examples already present in the dataset. In particular, repeatedly sampling or transforming the same small anomaly set does not necessarily expose the detector to new defect appearances, shapes, or locations. Augmentation must also be applied carefully to chip imagery, because strong geometric transformations can alter structural relationships that are fixed by the chip layout and imaging setup.

This motivates generative augmentation. Rather than only reweighting or transforming the observed anomalies, we aim to learn a generative model that can produce additional plausible variations of them. The objective is not to recover the unknown anomaly distribution exactly, which is impossible from a small finite sample, but to broaden the anomaly variation available during detector training. If the generated samples remain consistent with the real chip domain while varying defect appearance and location, they can provide supervision beyond the few anomalous images originally observed. When localization is considered, the generated anomaly should additionally be associated with a spatial mask that accurately represents its location.

Diffusion models911 are well suited to this setting because they provide a strong pretrained generative prior and can be adapted to new domains using relatively small datasets. Recent few-shot anomaly-generation methods use diffusion models to learn anomaly appearance, control anomaly location, or generate anomaly images and masks together. However, our chip images pose an additional difficulty: their visual structure is highly regular, with repeated layouts, sharp boundaries, narrow lines, and consistent geometry. Consequently, useful anomaly generation requires not only learning the defect distribution, but also preserving the underlying chip distribution.

Detail — the generative-augmentation view

Detection and generation address anomaly scarcity from different directions. A detector learns a decision function separating normal and anomalous images, whereas a generator learns a distribution from which additional anomalous samples can be produced. Writing pa(x) = p(x | y=a), the two objectives can be represented schematically as

detection:  fθ : x → [0,1]      generation:  pφ ≈ pa,   x̃ ~ pφ

Learning pa from only a small number of anomalous examples is strongly underdetermined. Diffusion therefore does not provide a mechanism for discovering completely unseen defect modes from nothing. Instead, its usefulness comes from combining the observed anomalies with a strong generative prior and producing additional plausible variations around the information available during training. In our setting, the original pretrained prior is first adapted using the much larger normal-chip dataset. Anomaly-specific adaptation is then performed on top of this domain-adapted model, so that the limited anomaly set primarily needs to describe the defect rather than simultaneously learning the entire chip domain.

1.2Formal problem statement

Let x ∈ RH×W×3 denote a chip inspection image and, when available, m ∈ {0,1}H×W denote its binary anomaly mask. We are given a large normal dataset and a substantially smaller anomalous dataset,

Dn = { xi }i=1..N ~ pn,    Da = { (xj, mj) } j=1..M ~ pa,    N ≫ M.

The normal dataset contains enough samples to characterize the visual structure of the chip domain, whereas the anomalous dataset contains much less information about individual defect types. We therefore separate learning the chip distribution from learning the anomaly distribution. The normal dataset is first used to adapt the pretrained diffusion model to the chip domain. The resulting domain-adapted model then serves as the initialization for anomaly-specific generation.

We write a general anomaly generator as

(x̃, m̃) = Gφ(z, C),    z ~ N(0,I),

where C denotes the conditioning information used by a particular generation strategy. Depending on the method, this condition may include a normal chip image, an anomaly mask, an anomaly category, or no explicit spatial condition. This formulation allows us to study direct anomaly generation, mask-conditioned generation, and joint image-mask generation within the same framework.

The generated samples should satisfy three practical requirements.

  1. Chip-domain fidelity. The generated image should preserve the structural characteristics of real chip images, including repeated layouts, sharp boundaries, line geometry, and other regular structures learned from the normal dataset. A visually apparent defect is not useful if the surrounding chip itself becomes unrealistic.
  2. Anomaly plausibility. The generated defect should resemble variations that could occur in real anomalous chip images rather than arbitrary perturbations introduced by the generator.
  3. Mask consistency. When a generation method produces a mask, the mask should correspond to the region in which the generated anomaly actually appears. Misalignment between image and mask introduces incorrect supervision for downstream localization.
Threat to validity

Synthetic samples may contain systematic artifacts that are absent from real defects. In that case, a detector could learn to recognize the generator rather than the anomaly characteristics of interest. Evaluating exclusively on held-out real images is therefore essential. Generated samples should additionally be inspected or filtered for cases in which the chip structure is unrealistic or no meaningful anomaly has actually been introduced.

1.3Summary

Chip anomaly generation requires learning both the underlying chip structure and the defect itself. Starting from a general pretrained diffusion model asks the small anomaly dataset to provide both. We instead use abundant normal images to learn the chip domain first, reserving the limited anomaly examples primarily for learning defects. Figure 1.5 summarizes this two-stage workflow.

  1. Normal-domain adaptation. Fine-tune the pretrained diffusion backbone on normal chip images using LoRA to learn layouts, boundaries, textures, and repeated structures. Fuse the trained LoRA into the backbone to initialize anomaly-specific training.
  2. Anomaly-specific adaptation. Investigate three strategies on this common domain-adapted backbone:
    • Direct anomaly generation. Generate complete anomalous chip images from noise, providing a baseline without explicit location control or corresponding masks.
    • Mask-first anomaly generation. Obtain a defect mask first, then condition image generation on it. A mask prior learned without the target chip layout can place a defect in an implausible region.
    • Coupled image-mask generation. Learn defect appearance and spatial support together. Our Approach 3 additionally anchors generation to a real normal chip and measures the delivered mask from the resulting image.

Across these strategies, useful samples must preserve chip-domain fidelity, depict plausible anomalies, and provide consistent masks where applicable. We inspect generated samples for structural errors and assess their value primarily through downstream detector performance on held-out real normal and anomalous images.

Part 02

Preliminaries

This part introduces the diffusion training and sampling processes, latent representations, and LoRA adaptation used in our workflow. It then discusses how noise level and the structure of chip imagery affect anomaly generation.

2.1The learned quantity

In the noise-prediction formulation considered here, a diffusion model learns to estimate the noise added to an image at a specified noise level. Training pairs are constructed by corrupting real images with sampled Gaussian noise. The network receives the corrupted image and its timestep, and predicts the noise used to create it9.

Generation applies the trained network repeatedly within a sampling algorithm. Starting from Gaussian noise, the sampler uses the predictions to move toward progressively cleaner states. The final state is a sample from the learned model; its quality depends on both the learned predictions and the sampling procedure.

Noise prediction is closely related to estimating the score: a direction in which the probability density of noisy data increases. This connection explains how training on corrupted examples can support generation, although the sampling update is more specific than simply subtracting the predicted noise.

Intuition

At high noise, many clean images could explain the same observation. As the noise level decreases, more image information becomes available and the model can refine its predictions. Training across noise levels prepares a single network for these different stages.

Detail — noise prediction as score estimation

Under the Gaussian forward process below, the optimal mean-squared-error noise predictor is the conditional mean E[ε | xt, t]. Its rescaling equals the score of the noised data marginal qt. A trained network approximates this relationship:

∇xt log qt(xt)  ≈  − εθ(xt, t) / 1 − αt

This is an identity for the optimal predictor, not a guarantee for a finite trained model. Reverse diffusion updates use the estimated score together with the noise schedule; depending on the sampler, the trajectory may be stochastic or deterministic.

Latent representation

In latent diffusion, an encoder maps a pixel image to a smaller learned representation. Noise addition and denoising operate in this latent space, and a decoder converts the final latent back into pixels11. During training, the path is image → encoder → noisy latent → denoiser. During generation, it is latent noise → repeated denoising → decoder → image.

The equations and chip drawings below use image-space notation for readability. For latent diffusion, replace the clean image x0 by its encoded representation and apply the same noise-prediction objective there. The encoder and decoder introduce a separate reconstruction limit, discussed in §2.5.

2.2Training objective

One training step, in full:

  1. Take a real image from the training set.
  2. Sample a timestep t from the training noise schedule.
  3. Generate random noise and mix it into the image at that level. Both the clean image and the exact noise are known to us.
  4. Show the network the noisy image and the value of t. Ask it to output the noise.
  5. Compare its answer to the noise actually added, and take a gradient step to reduce the difference.

Sampling different timesteps trains one network to handle different noise levels. Because the added noise is known, this objective supplies a direct regression target without adversarial training. It does not guarantee that additional optimization always improves generation: learning rate, data coverage, and overfitting remain important, especially during adaptation to a small anomaly set.

Detail — forward process and training objective

Let βt be the variance added at step t, αt = 1 − βt, and αt = ∏s≤t αs. The corrupted sample at any timestep can be drawn directly:

xt = αt x0  +  1 − αt ε,    ε ~ N(0, I)

Training therefore does not require traversing the full forward chain. With uniform timestep sampling, the simplified noise-prediction objective is

L(θ) = Ex0, ε, t ‖ ε − εθ(xt, t) ‖²

This simplified loss is related to a reweighted variational bound on data likelihood9. Changing the timestep sampling distribution changes the relative emphasis across noise levels unless compensated by loss weighting. Such choices should be evaluated against generation quality and downstream performance.

LoRA adaptation and fusion

LoRA adapts selected weight matrices by learning low-rank updates while keeping their base weights fixed14. This reduces the number of trainable parameters, but does not eliminate overfitting. In our first stage, these updates are trained on normal chip images using the diffusion objective. Fusion then adds the learned updates to the base weights, producing the domain-adapted backbone used to initialize anomaly-specific training.

Detail — low-rank weight update
W′ = W + λBA,   A ∈ Rr×k,   B ∈ Rd×r

For a base weight matrix W ∈ Rd×k, the rank r is small relative to d and k. The scale λ controls the update strength. Training adjusts A and B; fusion stores their scaled product in W′, so the adapted linear operation can be evaluated without separate LoRA branches.

2.3Sampling procedure

Sampling starts from Gaussian noise and uses the trained denoiser to construct a sequence of states at decreasing noise levels. At each step, the sampler combines the current state, the predicted noise, and coefficients determined by the schedule. This approximates the reverse of the corruption process learned during training.

Stochastic and deterministic samplers use the predictions differently. Standard DDPM sampling adds fresh noise at intermediate steps. Deterministic DDIM sampling uses a different update and introduces no additional randomness after the initial noise sample10. Different initial noise samples can still produce different images; deterministic sampling does not imply convergence to a single most likely image.

Conditional generation also supplies information such as a text embedding, anomaly category, or spatial mask. For classifier-free guidance, the model is trained to predict with and without the condition. At sampling time, a guidance weight combines these predictions to control the influence of the condition12. Stronger guidance can improve adherence while reducing diversity or introducing artifacts; spatial conditioning may also use mechanisms beyond this guidance weight.

Detail — sampler update and classifier-free guidance

The standard DDPM update below uses adjacent training timesteps. Here σt is the sampler standard deviation and z ~ N(0, I); the random term is omitted at the final step:

xt−1  =  ( 1 / αt ) · [  xt  −  ( (1−αt) / 1−αt ) · εθ(xt, t)  ]  +  σt z

DDIM uses a different combination of the predicted clean sample and predicted noise. Its deterministic form is not obtained by merely deleting the random term from this DDPM equation10. DDIM can also sample on a subsequence of the training timesteps. The number of steps is a quality–compute choice, not a fixed property of diffusion.

With a condition c, classifier-free guidance12 extrapolates away from the unconditional prediction:

ε̃ = εθ(xt, t, ∅) + w ( εθ(xt, t, c) − εθ(xt, t, ∅) )

Under this convention, w = 0 gives the unconditional prediction, w = 1 gives the conditional prediction, and w > 1 extrapolates beyond it. Increasing guidance often trades diversity for conditional fidelity12. For anomaly augmentation, tune this weight using a real validation set and reserve the held-out test set for final evaluation.

2.4The role of the timestep

The timestep identifies the noise level presented to the network. Its meaning depends on the schedule: the same numerical timestep need not represent the same signal-to-noise ratio in two different models.

As noise increases, image features become harder to distinguish. Their visibility depends on contrast, spatial extent, context, and the representation being noised. Coarse structure often becomes recognizable earlier in generation, while finer details are refined later9. This is a useful intuition, not a strict separation: a defect can affect both local detail and larger structures.

Consequence for this work

Low-noise steps may be particularly important for refining small defect details, while defect placement and larger structural changes may depend on earlier steps. This motivates examining layout fidelity and defect fidelity separately across noise levels. It does not establish that anomaly training should be restricted to the low-noise end of the schedule.

2.5Challenges specific to chip imagery

For the chip imagery considered here, successful generation must reproduce repeated structures while allowing localized defects. Four practical concerns follow from this combination.

a. Pretraining and chip-domain adaptation

A general pretrained diffusion model supplies useful image features, but its training distribution may differ substantially from the inspection setup. Accurate pad layouts, narrow lines, and consistent illumination are therefore adaptation targets rather than guaranteed properties of the initial model. This motivates learning the normal chip domain before using the scarce anomaly examples.

b. Sensitivity to geometric errors

Repeated chip structures provide a reference against which displacement and spacing errors can be visible. A small shift that is unobtrusive in an irregular scene may disrupt an otherwise regular pad array. Its practical significance depends on image resolution, registration, and inspection tolerances.

Relevant checks include spacing and alignment across repeated regions, boundary and line-width fidelity, and consistency with the imaging setup. Manufacturing geometry constrains the scene, but the recorded image also reflects optics, sampling, registration, and normal acquisition variation. Generated data should preserve that legitimate variation while avoiding spurious structural changes.

c. Global consistency without explicit constraints

Convolutions aggregate local information, while deeper layers and attention can model wider relationships. These mechanisms can learn regular geometry, but they do not by themselves enforce exact pitch or alignment. Small local spacing errors can coexist with a plausible texture and become apparent across a larger region. Figure 2.5 illustrates this possible failure pattern, rather than a necessary consequence of the architecture.

d. Preservation of defects through latent compression

The autoencoder introduced in §2.1 may attenuate small or low-contrast defects during compression and reconstruction. An 8× spatial reduction, for example, puts a few-pixel defect below the latent grid spacing, although the learned channels may still encode sub-grid detail. Spatial downsampling alone therefore does not prove that a defect is lost.

A useful diagnostic is to encode and decode real defect images without diffusion sampling, then inspect defect visibility and boundary alignment. Poor reconstruction identifies a representation bottleneck that denoiser fine-tuning alone may not resolve. Resolution, cropping, or autoencoder adaptation may need investigation before attributing the failure to anomaly-specific training.

Existing Anomaly-Generation Methods

3.1Method comparison

Recent few-shot anomaly-generation methods explore several ways of adding anomaly information to pretrained generative models. Representative approaches include AnomalyDiffusion 19, DualAnoDiff20, GroundingAnomaly21, TF-IDG22, MAGIC23, and Anodapter24. These methods differ primarily in how the diffusion model is adapted, how anomaly appearance and location are represented, and how the corresponding mask is obtained.

3.2Adaptation and conditioning strategies

At a high level, the surveyed methods can be grouped according to how anomaly information is introduced into the generation process.

  1. Conditioning-space adaptation. The diffusion backbone remains largely fixed while anomaly information is represented through learned embeddings. AnomalyDiffusion, for example, separates anomaly appearance from location through learned anomaly and spatial embeddings 19.
  2. Parameter-efficient diffusion adaptation. Trainable adapters, LoRA modules, or parallel branches are introduced into a pretrained diffusion model to learn anomaly-specific behavior. DualAnoDiff and Anodapter follow this general direction while using different mechanisms to relate anomaly appearance and spatial annotation 20 24.
  3. Conditional editing and exemplar transfer. Generation begins from a normal image and modifies a specified region using a mask, reference anomaly, or auxiliary spatial guidance. Such approaches preserve more of the original image explicitly, but their quality depends on both the spatial condition and the ability of the generator to blend the anomaly into the existing structure.

3.3AnomalyDiffusion: disentangled appearance and location

AnomalyDiffusion19 keeps the diffusion backbone entirely frozen and places the whole burden of adaptation on the conditioning. Its central idea is that an anomaly has two largely independent attributes — what it looks like and where it sits — and that these should be represented separately. Appearance is held in a small set of learnable text embeddings, one group per defect class, in the manner of textual inversion13. Location is supplied by a separate encoder that reads the anomaly mask and emits further embeddings occupying reserved positions in the same prompt. Together these form the spatial anomaly embedding.

Training updates the embeddings and mask encoder while retaining the pretrained denoiser. Generation starts from a real normal image and uses blended latent diffusion to preserve its unmasked content. In a separate training stage, textual inversion learns mask-generation embeddings from the annotation masks. These embeddings condition a frozen diffusion model to supply fresh masks at generation time.

The component that addresses mask alignment is adaptive attention re-weighting. During sampling the partially denoised image is compared against the normal source, and cross-attention toward the anomaly tokens is increased in regions where the two still agree — that is, where the anomaly has not yet formed. The correction is driven by the current generation state rather than by a fixed schedule, and it exists precisely because a mask given as conditioning does not by itself guarantee that the anomaly appears inside it.

Detail — AnomalyDiffusion training and sampling

Inputs and notation. Each training example contains an anomalous image x, its defect type i, and a binary mask m, with one inside the defect. Write z0 = E(x) for the VAE latent and mz for the mask resized to the latent grid. The three stages below separate learning defect appearance, learning mask shapes, and producing an image–mask pair19.

1. Learn appearance and spatial conditioning. Each defect type has learnable appearance tokens ea,i. A shared spatial encoder S maps the mask to location tokens es. Its ResNet-50 extracts features at several scales, an FPN combines them, and fully connected prediction heads map them into text-token space. Concatenating both token groups gives the denoiser's condition:

es = S(m),   c = concat(ea,i, es)
Lappearance = Ex,m,i,t,ε[‖mz ⊙ (ε − εθ(zt, t, c))‖22]

The mask weights the loss: only defect-region errors contribute directly to this objective. The noisy image still supplies context to the denoiser. The intended division is that appearance tokens identify the defect type, while mask-derived tokens specify its location.

  1. Sample an image–mask pair and apply matching spatial augmentations.
  2. Encode the image, sample a timestep and Gaussian noise, and form zt using the forward process in §2.
  3. Encode the mask, concatenate the conditioning tokens, and predict the added noise.
  4. Backpropagate the masked error into the appearance tokens and spatial encoder; keep the diffusion backbone fixed.

2. Learn a separate mask-generation condition. Here the training target is the mask image itself. Encode it as u0 = E(m), add noise to obtain ut, and optimize mask tokens em,i with the ordinary denoising objective:

Lmask = Em,i,t,ε[ ‖ε − εθ(ut, t, em,i)‖22]

Only the mask tokens are updated in this stage. The whole mask image, including its zero-valued region, is part of the target. Thus em,i generates a new mask, whereas S(m) describes a mask already chosen for image generation. The paper uses eight appearance tokens, four spatial tokens, and four mask-generation tokens.

3. Sample a mask, then blend an anomaly into a normal image. Sample a mask using em,i, choose a normal image y, and construct c from its defect type and sampled mask. After each reverse step, retain the generated latent inside the mask and the appropriately noised normal-image latent outside it:

zt−1 = mz ⊙ zt−1generated + (1 − mz) ⊙ zt−1normal

Adaptive attention re-weighting (AAR). During sampling, decode the current clean-image estimate and compare it with y inside the mask. A small difference indicates that the intended anomaly is still weak. AAR converts these differences into spatial weights using an inverse-difference mapping and a softmax, allocating more attention to weakly changed regions. The mask-area multiplier normalizes the total weight. With Q from U-Net features and K, V from the conditioning tokens, the operation is schematically:

A = softmax(QKT / √d),   A′ = A ⊙ wm,   output = A′V

The spatial weights are resized and broadcast to match the attention map. This sampling correction encourages the defect to fill the mask; it does not retrain the model. The final output pairs the decoded image with the mask used to guide it. For chip data, this leaves two separate checks: whether the visible defect fills that mask and whether latent encoding and blending preserve the surrounding fine geometry.

3.4DualAnoDiff: parallel whole-image and defect-only branches

DualAnoDiff20 attacks mask alignment from the opposite direction. Rather than conditioning the image on a mask, it generates two images at once from one backbone carrying two LoRA14 branches: one branch produces the complete anomalous image, the other produces the anomalous region in isolation. The prompts are nested so that the concept named in the defect-only branch is the same concept embedded in the whole-image branch.

The branches are not independent. Their self-attention is coupled, so each branch attends over the other's features as well as its own. This couples the two descriptions of the defect, although it does not guarantee perfect agreement. The mask is then obtained by segmenting the defect-only image, which is comparatively easy because that image contains the defect against a plain field. A background compensation module further supplies information from an image with the foreground object removed, injecting that context into the whole-image branch's self-attention to reduce object distortion and mixing between the object and scene background.

The ordering is worth noting for what follows: the mask here is derived after the image, from a second view of the same defect, rather than given to the generator in advance.

Detail — DualAnoDiff branches and feature interaction

Two targets from one annotation. Given an anomalous image x and its defect mask ma, construct an isolated defect image xa = x ⊙ ma. It retains the original image coordinates, with pixels outside the defect set to zero. The global branch learns x; the anomaly branch learns xa. Both use a pretrained diffusion backbone with separate LoRA parameters20.

Nested prompts. The global prompt has the form a [object] with [defect], while the anomaly prompt contains [defect]. The shared defect descriptor relates the isolated target to the defect within the full image. A trainable text encoder supplies the two text conditions cg and ca.

One training iteration. Encode both targets with the VAE, sample one common timestep t, and noise the two latents with εg and εa. The noise draws need not be identical. Run both denoisers at that timestep, exchanging features through SAIM, and minimize their summed prediction errors. Writing ε̂g and ε̂a for the two predictions gives:

Ldual = E[ ‖εg − ε̂g‖22 + ‖εa − ε̂a‖22]

Each prediction depends on its own noisy latent and text condition, as well as the exchanged features. Training fits the LoRA branches, text conditioning, and interaction modules while keeping the base diffusion weights frozen. The two targets teach complementary tasks: placing a defect within the whole scene and representing the same defect separately enough to extract its annotation.

Self-attention Interaction Module (SAIM). Let Hg, Ha have shape B × N × C, with N spatial positions. After attention blocks, SAIM pairs the streams at corresponding positions and rearranges them to (BN) × 2 × C. Attention mixes the two branch features at each position, followed by a residual addition and a split back into the original streams:

H = pair(Hg, Ha)
H′ = H + SelfAttn(H)
(H′g, H′a) = unpair(H′)

This preserves the coordinate correspondence of the two targets. The next U-Net blocks receive the updated features, allowing the global context and local anomaly representation to influence each other throughout denoising.

Background Compensation Module (BCM). An object segmenter provides a foreground mask mf, distinct from the defect mask ma. Removing the entire object gives xb = (1 − mf) ⊙ x. A background diffusion path processes its noised latent without SAIM. Its self-attention features Hb are fused into the global stream when computing keys and values:

Q = HgWQ,   Hfused = Hg + γ MLP(Hb)
K = HfusedWK,   V = HfusedWV

Here γ is learned. Queries retain the global-stream features; keys and values incorporate background context. This gives the model both scene information and the outline left by the removed object.

Sampling and mask extraction. Denoise the two streams together with their nested prompts, SAIM, and BCM background conditioning. Decode both images and segment the isolated anomaly image using a model such as SAM or U2-Net. Pair this mask with the global image. The annotation mask is therefore required to construct training targets, but its generated counterpart is obtained after sampling. For chip imagery, the relevant checks are agreement between branches, segmentation of small defects, and whether BCM's foreground-object distinction is useful for the inspection crop.

3.5MAGIC: inpainting with deliberately injected diversity

MAGIC23 starts from a Stable Diffusion inpainting backbone fine-tuned with DreamBooth16 on the few available defects, and confines generation to a masked region of a normal image. A central concern is diversity: a model fitted to a dozen examples tends to reproduce those examples, and the resulting synthetic set adds little that a detector has not already seen. Three mechanisms address diversity, background fidelity, and plausible defect placement.

Gaussian noise is added to the anomaly prompt embedding during both training and sampling, so the model sees a continuous neighbourhood of embeddings rather than a few isolated points. The classifier-free guidance scale12 is varied spatially, starting lower inside the anomaly region to permit textural variety and increasing during denoising, while remaining high outside it. And the mask itself — generated by textual inversion rather than supplied — is repositioned before inpainting using keypoints selected from a reference annotation and matched between the reference anomaly image and the target normal image, so that defects land at semantically plausible places on the object rather than wherever the mask generator happened to put them.

For our chip imagery, a concern is whether semantic matching can identify the correct location among repeated structures. When the inspected surface fills the frame, foreground segmentation also provides little restriction on defect placement. The usefulness of this relocation step therefore needs to be evaluated for the chip layout and crop being used.

Detail — MAGIC inpainting, guidance, and mask alignment

Inpainting training. For an anomalous training image x and defect mask m, remove the defect region to form b = (1 − m) ⊙ x. The denoiser receives the noisy full-image latent, the resized mask, and E(b) as concatenated channels. The target remains the noise added to E(x). Using ε-prediction notation, the core fine-tuning objective is:

ztinp = concat(zt, mz, E(b))
Linpaint = E[ ‖ε − εθ(ztinp, t, c̃)‖22]

The mask is an input condition; the core loss above is measured over the full latent. DreamBooth fine-tuning updates the inpainting model to reconstruct the few observed defects from their surrounding context. The released training code makes this conditioning and loss construction explicit.

Prompt perturbation during training and sampling. Gaussian prompt perturbation (GPP) adds noise to the encoded prompt:

c̃ = τ(p) + δ,   δ ∼ N(0, σ2I)

Training uses perturbed conditions, and inference draws fresh perturbations. This changes the text condition independently of the initial diffusion noise. The training and inference implementations expose this perturbation through a text-noise scale. Conceptually, repeated training examples are presented under nearby conditions, giving sampling a neighbourhood to explore rather than a single embedding for each prompt.

Separate mask-shape training. MAGIC also applies GPP to textual inversion on mask images: train mask tokens with Gaussian perturbations while freezing that generator, then sample with perturbed tokens. The sampled mask supplies a shape; CAMA determines its placement on the target image23.

Spatially adaptive guidance (SAG). Let εc and εu denote the predictions with and without the anomaly text condition. Use a spatial scale map instead of one scalar CFG weight:

w(r) = mz win(r) + (1 − mz) wout
εguided = εu + w(r) ⊙ (εc − εu)

Here r denotes sampling progress from the initial noise toward the final image. The paper describes increasing the inner scale along a cosine schedule while keeping the outer scale fixed23. The repository's example configuration uses inner bounds of 4.0 and 7.5 and an outer scale of 7.5. These are example settings, not constants implied by CFG. The spatial map lets defect texture and surrounding content receive different guidance strengths during the same reverse step.

Mask alignment before inpainting (CAMA). Start with a sampled mask mgen, a target normal image y, and a reference anomaly pair (xref, mref). The paper's alignment procedure is23:

  1. Select the reference mask's centroid and upper/lower boundary points on the same vertical line.
  2. Match these locations from xref to y using semantic image features. The upper/lower matches define a candidate line; select a center on that line with high centroid similarity inside the target foreground.
  3. Translate mgen so its centroid reaches that center, then intersect it with the target foreground mask.

The reference pair supplies placement cues while the sampled mask supplies shape. Inpainting then uses the target normal image, the aligned mask, a perturbed prompt, and SAG. The output annotation is this aligned mask. For our evaluation, placement, visible mask coverage, and texture diversity should be assessed separately: a plausible location alone does not establish that the intended defect was rendered.

Separating location from appearance gives explicit spatial control. Keeping both outputs makes their disagreement inspectable. Part 5 develops the training, blending, residual labeling and cleanup used in our build.

4.4Normal structure as a condition

The normal chip contributes information that a defect category cannot: where this sample's pads, lanes and boundaries actually lie. Our build uses that image both to guide mask placement and to preserve the background during anomaly synthesis. Part 05

Anomaly Generation Conditioned on Normal Chip Images

5.1Method overview

Our implementation belongs to Approach 3, the coupled image-mask family introduced in §4.3. It follows Anodapter24: a single diffusion backbone is trained to produce both the anomalous image and its segmentation mask, with the two concepts held apart by rare text tokens rather than by separate models. A mask is an ordinary image as far as a latent diffusion model is concerned, so both distributions can occupy one set of weights. At generation the image is spatially conditioned on the mask the model has just produced, which makes the relationship explicit even though it does not guarantee perfect pixel-level alignment.

The final presentation organizes our additions into four components: pseudo-normal filling supplies a matching background during image training; latent blending preserves the normal reference during sampling; residual labeling measures the image change; and component cleanup makes the proposal usable before painting. All use the abundance of normal chip structure. Sections 5.3–5.5 describe these components in detail (presentation slides 37–38 and 63–68)25.

In plain terms

One model learns to draw defects and to propose where a defect should go. At generation it proposes a region first, then paints a defect onto a real defect-free chip image. The proposal and the delivered mask remain separate quantities, so their agreement can be inspected.

5.2Backbone adaptation and concept separation

Detail — normal-domain training before anomaly training

Stage 1 uses 5,000 normal chip images. The baseline freezes the VAE and both SDXL text encoders, and trains a U-Net LoRA under a fixed normal-chip prompt. Each training step encodes a normal image, adds noise at a sampled timestep, and fits the noise predictor. This stage learns the domain before scarce anomaly examples are introduced (presentation slides 21–24 and 56–57)25.

Negative prompting at sampling. A positive prompt describes chip structure, while the negative prompt names undesirable image qualities such as blur, painterly texture, and wavy routing. Under standard CFG, the negative-prompt prediction replaces the empty-prompt baseline:

εCFG = εnegative + w(εpositive − εnegative)

This changes sampling without retraining. For anomaly generation, the negative prompt must preserve the requested defect concept: terms such as broken routing or distorted geometry may describe the intended anomaly. The current anomaly configuration therefore uses image-quality negatives, as discussed in §5.5.

Cluster conditioning. Normal images include several layout families. The experiment clusters their VAE embeddings and learns a token set for each cluster alongside the LoRA, with the existing text encoder weights frozen. Selecting a cluster at sampling supplies a layout-family condition. This can reduce mixing between families, but it does not enforce exact pad pitch or line alignment. The normal-only measurements are reported in §6.2.

Stage 2 initializes the anomaly methods from normal-domain-adapted weights. The presentation notes that MAGIC requires an inpainting U-Net, which was also adapted. Thus these experiments share a domain-adaptation strategy, rather than an identical architecture in every row. Cluster conditioning is a studied variant; it is not evidence that every anomaly configuration enables cluster tokens.

The backbone is a latent diffusion model11 of the Stable Diffusion XL family. It is first specialised to the chip domain by fusing a LoRA14 adapter trained on normal images alone, after which a second LoRA is attached for the anomaly stage together with two lightweight adapter networks that supply spatial conditioning. Only these added parameters are trained; the base weights stay frozen. With on the order of ten annotated defects per class, a full fine-tune risks overfitting and weakening the normal-domain adaptation.

The two concepts are separated by rare tokens with weak prior associations in the text encoder, in the manner of textual inversion13: one token binds the anomalous image, another binds the mask, and a per-class token distinguishes defect types. Training binds these tokens to the required image, mask, and defect concepts. Normal images are included in training with an empty mask and the mask token alone, which is what teaches the model that the absence of a type token means the absence of a defect.

Detail — the training objective

Each step encodes the anomalous image and its mask into latents and stacks them into a single batch, so that one forward pass covers both branches. Writing the two halves of the predicted noise as ε̂img and ε̂msk, the objective is the sum of two ordinary denoising terms:

L = ‖ε̂img − εimg‖2  +  ‖ε̂msk − εmsk‖2

The two halves are conditioned differently — different prompt, different spatial input — but share the diffusion backbone and its LoRA. Two separate T2I-Adapters inject the branch-specific spatial features into the U-Net down blocks.

5.3Conditioning on a real normal die

In the published method the image branch receives only the mask, and must therefore synthesise the entire frame. On a chip surface that is the dominant failure mode: the periodic bump array is both the hardest thing for a generative model to reproduce and the thing already available in thousands of real examples. The image branch here is given a real defect-free die alongside the mask, so its task reduces to painting the defect and leaving the rest unchanged.

Training this requires a die that corresponds to the anomalous image, and no before-and-after pairs exist — no die was photographed before it cracked. The conditioning die is therefore a pseudo-normal reconstruction of the anomalous image itself: the defect region is replaced with surface copied from one lattice period away, exploiting the periodicity of the die. Pairing an anomalous image with an unrelated normal die would make the background condition less predictive of the target, encouraging the model to ignore that channel.

Matching the background reduces how much structure the image task must invent. It does not make the background's noise-prediction loss or gradient identically zero: the denoiser still predicts noise over the complete target latent, and the VAE can mix information across nearby pixels. The benefit is a more informative condition; the explicit sampling constraint comes from blending.

Conditioning alone is a prior the model may disregard, so it is paired with an arithmetic constraint during sampling: at every denoising step the latents outside the mask are overwritten with those of the real die, noised to the current step. The model's latent trajectory is constrained toward the real surface outside the permitted region. A small dilation of that region is deliberate — a real defect casts a shadow and interrupts the lattice row it occupies slightly beyond its own annotation, and a mask boundary enforced exactly produces defects that stop abruptly at the annotation edge.

Detail — the blending update

With m the mask resampled to the latent grid, zdie the encoded die and λ a blending strength, each step follows the ordinary sampler update and is then corrected toward the die outside the mask:

zt ← zt + λ(1 − m) • (ztdie − zt)

At λ = 1 the region outside the mask becomes the die exactly; at λ = 0 the constraint is absent and the model generates the whole frame, recovering the published behaviour. Inside the mask the factor is zero at every value of λ, so the defect itself is never pulled toward the die.

Threat to validity

The conditioning die differs slightly between training and generation. In training it is a reconstruction, carrying a filled patch exactly where the defect belongs; at generation it is an untouched die with no such patch. A model that learned to read the filled patch as a cue for where to paint would generate weak defects when the cue is absent. The mask channel makes that cue redundant, and normal training images contain no filled patch at all, but the symptom to monitor is generated defects appearing consistently fainter than those the model reconstructs on training images.

5.4Derivation of the delivered mask

Because a mask is generated as an image, it must survive an encode and decode through the autoencoder. At the working resolution the latent grid is eight times coarser than the image. A hairline defect only a few pixels wide can return as a soft, widened, or interrupted ridge, and thresholding then decides its silhouette. This is a reconstruction concern to measure, rather than a strict minimum defect width implied by the latent-cell size.

The delivered mask is instead measured from the difference between the decoded image and its normal reference, evaluated on the full pixel grid. This avoids relying on the outline decoded by the mask branch, although image reconstruction and residual processing still affect the result. The mask branch continues to run and continues to condition the image; it simply returns to the task it performs well, which is deciding where a defect should appear rather than describing its outline.

Differencing is performed against the die passed through the same autoencoder, which reduces shared reconstruction error before thresholding. It does not remove that error perfectly. The result is confined to a dilation of the proposal, because decoder coupling can spread a small perturbation beyond the region that was directly edited.

The proposal and delivered mask represent different stages and may not agree exactly. The image branch can fill only part of the proposed region or paint a shadow slightly outside it. Both masks are retained so that this difference can be inspected.

Detail — proposal cleanup, illustrated

A decoded mask can contain disconnected pieces of one crack as well as small isolated regions. The three proposal_* controls answer different questions: which pieces belong together, which regions are large enough, and how many eligible regions should remain? The following schematic follows the documented proposal flow:

decoded mask → threshold → close gaps → remove small components → retain the K largest → P

proposal_close: connect before counting. Morphological closing is dilation followed by erosion with a structuring element, as illustrated in the OpenCV morphology documentation. The dilation temporarily expands foreground regions; the erosion contracts the outer boundary while some filled gaps remain connected. The footprint's size and shape determine which gaps can close.

The useful scale is one that bridges interruptions within a defect while preserving separation from neighbouring defects. In this example, too small a scale lets one crack consume three slots in the component cap; too large a scale makes two distinct regions consume one slot. Closing therefore changes the interpretation of proposal_max_components, as well as the proposal's shape. The configured value 128 is an image-space length scale, whereas the area threshold below is a pixel count. It should not be read as a guaranteed 128-pixel bridge distance; kernel geometry matters.

proposal_min_area: measure occupied pixels. Count the foreground pixels in each connected component after closing. A minimum of 256 does not mean a required width of 256 pixels, nor a minimum bounding-box area. For intuition, a solid 16 × 16 patch contains 256 pixels, while a 150 × 2 strip contains 300. The strip can survive even though it is only two pixels wide. A shorter genuine crack can fall below the same cutoff and be removed.

proposal_max_components: select among survivors. Rank the eligible components by their foreground area and retain at most five in the current configuration. This favors large regions, not necessarily the most realistic ones. If only two survive the area filter, the proposal contains two; the cap does not synthesize three more. Setting it to null removes this ranking cutoff while leaving the other cleanup operations in place.

Reading failures through the three controls. Several fragments of one visible crack suggest inspecting closing first. Many tiny isolated regions suggest inspecting the minimum area. Too many spatially distinct, otherwise plausible regions suggest inspecting the count cap. If the largest region itself is implausible, a smaller cap may keep that region and discard better ones; ranking cannot correct poor placement.

Pixel-based settings also depend on resolution. If both image axes are rescaled by a factor s, a comparable geometric starting point scales lengths by s and areas by s2; K remains a count. The figures illustrate the operators rather than implementation details: the configuration alone does not specify the closing kernel footprint, connectivity convention, or exact residual reduction.

Evaluation

A synthetic anomaly is useful only if it transfers to real inspection data. We therefore evaluate the generator at three levels: similarity in the diffusion model's latent space, separability by a real-versus-synthetic classifier, and downstream anomaly-detection performance on held-out real images.

6.1Three evaluation questions

The first two tests describe the generated distribution using VAE features. The third tests the purpose of the project directly with a DenseNet-121 detector and identical real validation and test sets across training compositions. The final presentation supplies the protocols and results below.25

6.2Latent-space similarity and synthetic detection

For the latent-space test, the same VAE used by the diffusion pipeline encodes real and synthetic images. We compare the resulting embedding distributions with symmetric KL divergence, directional KL divergence and latent Fréchet distance where reported. Smaller values indicate closer distributions, but neither metric proves that the samples are useful for training a detector.

The second test trains a linear SVM on the same latent features to distinguish real from synthetic images, with repeated train/validation/test splits. For a balanced binary test, 0.5 is chance accuracy; the real-versus-real split provides an empirical reference close to that value. This tests distinguishability by this classifier in this representation, rather than proving visual equivalence.

Detail — data reuse within training and separation from evaluation

In the n = 20 example on slide 41, the same 20 real anomalies train the diffusion model and enter detector training. The remaining real anomalies are split into validation and test sets. Real normal images also have disjoint training, validation and test partitions. Kept synthetic samples augment detector training only; rejected samples are discarded.25

Reusing a real anomaly for both training stages is part of the intended data budget. The essential separation is from held-out evaluation data, with identical validation and test examples across real-only, augmented and filtered-augmentation runs.

The filter removes samples with an empty delivered mask or with no image change inside the proposed defect region. Both cases assign an anomaly label to an image that remains effectively normal, which would provide contradictory supervision.

6.4Validity and reporting limits

Threat to validity

A synthetic detector can separate real and generated images because of a background artifact that a latent metric does not isolate. Conversely, similar latent distributions can coexist with poor masks or weak defect supervision. The downstream test is therefore the primary result, and it must use held-out real anomalies with a fixed detector protocol.

The current results should be extended with repeated seeds, confidence intervals or bootstrap intervals, per-defect-type breakdowns, and direct inspection of false positives and false negatives. Balanced accuracy is especially important at small anomaly budgets because AUROC and F1 can look respectable while the operating threshold remains close to chance.

Together, the FM and WC experiments support using synthetic data where real defects are scarce, with benefits depending on defect type and the strength of the real-only baseline. Part 5 explains the generator used here; Part 7 develops the proposed improvements to speed, layout modelling and paired conditioning.

Part 07

Future Research

The final presentation proposes three directions: faster generation, broader chip-domain modelling, and an explicit two-model pipeline that conditions both defect location and appearance on the same normal chip. These are research plans, separate from the results in Part 6.25

The first priority is to reduce the cost of generating an image–mask pair. The presentation proposes compiling the denoising and VAE computation, then investigating flow-matching or few-step models if execution improvements alone are insufficient. Detail — a useful latency comparison

Measure compilation and warm-up separately from steady-state execution. Profile the mask pass, image pass, VAE encoding and decoding, and residual-mask cleanup so the change addresses the actual bottleneck. The PyTorch compilation tutorial provides the implementation starting point.

Compare end-to-end latency and peak memory at the same resolution, batch size, hardware, precision and sampling configuration. Retain checks of chip geometry, defect visibility, mask alignment and downstream usefulness: a faster generator is useful only if its training samples retain their value.

Flow matching and few-step generation should be evaluated as separate model or training choices, rather than assumed to be drop-in scheduler replacements for the current SDXL checkpoint. The presentation reports no measured result for either direction.

7.2Broader chip data and scalable models

A longer-term direction is a chip-domain foundation model trained on more normal images and a wider range of layouts. The present experiments suggest that learning defect appearance is easier than preserving repeated pads, straight lanes and exact spacing. More data should therefore broaden coverage of layout families, rather than merely add similar examples from the same family.

The presentation also proposes comparing the SDXL U-Net with a diffusion transformer (DiT). DiT processes latent patches with transformer blocks and studies scaling through model size and token count.26 This offers a different route to larger chip models, but does not itself guarantee straight lines or exact periodicity. Whether scaling improves inspection geometry remains an experimental question.

Detail — separating layout coverage from geometric fidelity

A useful evaluation would hold out entire layout families as well as individual images. This distinguishes reproduction of familiar layouts from transfer to new chip structures. Compare pad spacing, row alignment, lane continuity, boundary shape and unintended defects alongside latent distances and downstream detection.

Check the VAE reconstruction floor described in §2.5 before attributing every geometric error to the denoiser. Increasing denoiser capacity cannot recover information that the chosen representation consistently loses. These checks are a proposed evaluation plan, not results supplied by the presentation.

7.3Paired normal-conditioned location and appearance

The proposed two-model pipeline makes normal-chip conditioning explicit at both stages. First recover a pseudo-normal chip from each real anomaly and its annotation. Then train a mask diffusion model to place a defect on that chip, and an image diffusion model to render the defect using the same chip and mask. Each training example becomes a matching triplet: recovered normal image, true mask and original anomalous image.

At inference, one normal chip conditions both models. The mask model samples a location compatible with its layout; the image model uses that sampled mask to generate appearance. This is still a mask-then-image procedure. Its key change from Approach 2 is that location is conditioned on the particular chip, addressing the layout mismatch identified in §4.2.

Detail — training targets, sampling and the difference from our build

Let xa be a real anomaly, m its annotation and c its defect class. Normal recovery produces x̂n, giving the triplet (x̂n, m, xa). The two diffusion models use different denoising targets but share the clean normal condition:

Mask diffusionRecovered normal chip, defect classTrue annotation mask

m ∼ pθ(m | xn, c),   xa ∼ pφ(xa | xn, m, c).

At inference the recovered training condition is replaced by a real or generated normal chip. Independent noise inputs let mask placement and image appearance vary while both models see the same layout. A generated normal input also inherits the normal generator's geometric errors.

The current build already conditions mask generation on a real normal chip and uses pseudo-normal filling for the anomalous image branch. However, it shares a U-Net across tasks, and the supplied training description does not establish a recovered, matching normal counterpart for every mask-training target. The proposed change is two explicit conditional models trained from matching recovered triplets.

The proposed output is the generated image together with its sampled mask. Conditioning encourages correspondence but cannot guarantee pixel-level agreement. Compare the mask with the visible image change and retain residual-based checks from the current method as an alignment diagnostic during evaluation.

Threat to validity

Normal recovery is the central prerequisite. A scar left by filling can reveal the original mask location, allowing the mask model to exploit a reconstruction artifact. Recovered training backgrounds may also differ from real normal chips at inference. Both effects require inspection before an apparent gain can be attributed to learning valid defect locations.

A direct comparison should keep real-anomaly budgets, data splits and the downstream detector fixed across the current and proposed pipelines, then assess geometry, image–mask agreement, generation time and usefulness on held-out real defects. This would test whether matching the normal condition improves the supervision that synthetic samples provide.

Glossary

Timestep t
The noise level, from 0 (clean) to T (pure static). Fed to the network as an input.
Noise schedule ᾱt
How much of the original image survives at level t. Falls from 1 to 0.
Denoiser εθ
The trained network. Given a noisy image and its noise level, it names the noise.
Latent space
A compressed representation, typically 8× smaller per side, that diffusion actually runs in.
Guidance weight
How hard generation is pushed to obey its conditioning. High values trade diversity for obedience.
Warm start
Beginning a fine-tune from weights already fitted to related data, rather than from scratch.
Low-rank update
A deliberately small set of trainable parameters added to a frozen model — few enough to fit from few examples.
Mode collapse
A generator that produces near-copies of a handful of training examples instead of varied samples.
Escape / overkill
A defective part passed as good / a good part rejected as defective.
Pitch
The centre-to-centre spacing of a repeated feature, such as a pad array. Fixed by the mask set.
